# Design styles: a vocabulary, not a preset menu

Style names describe families of visual decisions. They do not determine usability, product strategy, or accessibility. A style is successful when its typography, color, composition, imagery, materials, motion, and content work together for the product and audience. Avoid applying a named style as a superficial skin.

For any style, define what the style means in the actual product, which elements carry it, what must remain conventional, and how it behaves across content, states, and devices. A product can blend compatible traits, but record the reason; indiscriminate mixing creates visual noise.

## Editorial

**Traits:** strong typographic hierarchy, controlled reading measure, varied pacing, image/caption relationships, clear source and metadata, intentional whitespace. **Useful for:** journalism, essays, research, catalogs, long-form product narratives. **Risks:** overly dramatic display type, weak scanability, inaccessible small captions, imagery mistaken for evidence. **Keep:** clear navigation, source/date/author context, responsive text, and readable body copy.

## Swiss / International typographic

**Traits:** grid discipline, asymmetric balance, precise alignment, restrained palette, typography as structure, explicit hierarchy. **Useful for:** information-rich services, professional tools, data communication. **Risks:** rigidity, impersonal tone, overreliance on thin type or tiny labels, grid conventions overriding content. **Keep:** humane language, adaptable layouts, and sufficient contrast.

## Minimal / quiet

**Traits:** few elements, limited color, clear spacing, reduced ornament, focused interactions. **Useful for:** focused tasks, calm brands, tools where content should dominate. **Risks:** hidden controls, low-contrast affordances, empty screens, ambiguous icons, sparse hierarchy mistaken for clarity. **Keep:** visible action labels, discoverable navigation, adequate feedback, and accessible contrast.

## Brutalist / raw digital

**Traits:** visible structure, bold typography, hard edges, strong contrast, raw or intentionally unpolished materials. **Useful for:** cultural/editorial identities, experimental portfolios, communities where a deliberately unconventional tone is authentic. **Risks:** inaccessible color/typography, confusing hierarchy, hostile forms, novelty that blocks core tasks. **Keep:** stable interaction conventions and semantic structure even when the visual surface is unconventional.

## Neo-industrial / utilitarian

**Traits:** modular panels, technical typography, status colors, dense information, visible controls, mechanical/material cues. **Useful for:** operations, engineering, logistics, monitoring tools. **Risks:** faux cockpit dashboards, excessive labels, alarm fatigue, illegible density, ornamental pseudo-data. **Keep:** real operational priorities, severity levels, meaningful units, and quiet default states.

## Luxury / premium restraint

**Traits:** spacious composition, precise typography, high-quality imagery/material cues, measured motion, limited but intentional accents. **Useful for:** premium services, hospitality, crafted goods, financial or professional services when the brand genuinely supports it. **Risks:** elitism, inaccessible low contrast, tiny text, slow experiences, vague copy or empty luxury clichés. **Keep:** transparent pricing/terms, practical task support, performance, and accessible type.

## Corporate / institutional

**Traits:** clear information architecture, neutral palette, consistent components, formal tone, predictable actions. **Useful for:** enterprise, government, education, regulated or multi-role services. **Risks:** generic stock aesthetic, dense bureaucracy, confusing organizational terminology, overstandardized pages. **Keep:** plain user language, service outcomes, trustworthy status, and a distinct but restrained brand.

## Futuristic / speculative

**Traits:** luminous accents, advanced data visualization, spatial or kinetic forms, dark surfaces, technical imagery. **Useful for:** genuine technology storytelling, science, creative tools, immersive products. **Risks:** low contrast, fake complexity, sci-fi decoration, poor readability, motion overload, false claims of capability. **Keep:** conventional controls, clear units and status, accessible alternatives, and evidence-based claims.

## Soft / friendly / playful

**Traits:** warm colors, rounded forms, approachable illustrations, conversational copy, lively feedback. **Useful for:** learning, consumer products, wellness or community contexts when the tone respects user needs. **Risks:** childishness, trivializing serious subjects, unclear controls, excessive bounce, color-only status. **Keep:** adult respect, clear consequence, restrained motion, and seriousness where risk requires it.

## Organic / human / tactile

**Traits:** natural forms, warm surfaces, photography or illustration with material character, hand-crafted detail. **Useful for:** community, food, nature, craft, care, local services. **Risks:** stereotyped imagery, low contrast, uneven consistency, metaphors that do not translate culturally. **Keep:** honest representation, robust tokens, accessible controls, and authentic assets.

## Neo-brutal / high-contrast web expression

**Traits:** flat surfaces, bold outlines, oversized type, assertive blocks, vivid accent colors. **Useful for:** independent creative brands or campaigns where directness is intentional. **Risks:** harsh color combinations, visually noisy pages, inconsistent responsive behavior, all-elements-equal emphasis. **Keep:** restrained accent use, readable content, clear hierarchy, and tested controls.

## Style selection method

1. Identify product task, audience, content, risk, and brand evidence.
2. Choose two or three style dimensions, not a label alone (e.g., “editorial hierarchy + calm utilitarian density”).
3. Translate them into typography, palette, grid, surfaces, imagery, and motion decisions.
4. Define what should remain conventional for usability/accessibility.
5. Create a representative screen and test both task clarity and visual identity.
6. Remove style elements that are decorative, inaccessible, misleading, or inconsistent with the product.

The same style can be expressed differently by culture, language, platform, and product. Do not assign emotional meaning to colors or materials as universal fact. Validate with intended users and actual content. A style catalogue is inspiration, not research evidence.
